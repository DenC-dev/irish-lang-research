Síneadh Findings Evolution Gaps Limits Reproduce

Síneadh

Irish, noun: lengthening, extension. The síneadh fada is the length mark on a vowel.

What can computational linguistics say about Irish learned in Irish-medium schools and Irish written online, and where does the evidence stop?

Read the working paper (PDF) Code and data pointers

Status: undergraduate working paper, version 0.3 (16 November 2026). Not peer reviewed, and not yet checked by a fluent Irish speaker. Literature search, code and drafting were done with an AI assistant under the author's direction.

Three pilot findings

Distance from the written standard is easy to measure

Each result comes from running the same transparent rules over two public treebanks: 4,910 sentences of edited Irish and 2,596 Irish tweets posted from 2009 to 2019.

After mo or do, a noun that should be lenited is written unlenited in 18 of 177 tweet contexts and 3 of 474 edited ones. Eclipsis shows the same direction (5.0% against 0.2%).

Words missing a length mark fell by two thirds between the earlier and later samples, and English tokens fell from 6.6% to 5.2%. Emoji went up.

The samples overlap in time and were drawn differently.

Subordinate clauses per ten sentences or tweets. Relative clauses are about three times as frequent in edited text.

A register effect: short text hides syntax.

What these do not show: who wrote the tweets. No public corpus of online Irish records whether a writer learned Irish at home, in an Irish-medium school, or as an adult. An unmarked mutation can come from fast typing, autocorrect, an English noun, dialect, informality or incomplete acquisition.

How it has evolved

Four things changed at different speeds

Most confusion about "new Irish" comes from mixing up the written norm, the schools, the spoken language and the tools.

Norm and schools

  1. 1958Official written standard published, then left almost unrevised until 2012 and 2017.
  2. 197211 primary and 5 post-primary Irish-medium schools outside the Gaeltacht.
  3. 2019/20Enrolment outside the Gaeltacht peaks at 48,518 pupils.
  4. 2023/2446,933 pupils, the first fall in fifty years.
  5. 2025First State policy for Irish-medium education outside the Gaeltacht. Northern Ireland reaches 7,811 pupils, up from 1,602 in 2001.

Tools

  1. 2000sRule-based spelling and grammar checkers; a 30-million-word corpus.
  2. 2008–09First Irish predictive text on phones.
  3. 2015–22Annotated tweet corpora, a tweet treebank, a monolingual BERT model.
  4. 2024First Irish-adapted large language model; national corpus of 101 million words.
  5. 2025–26Evaluation turn: on one grammar benchmark fluent humans score 90.1% and the best model 73.5%.

The tools moved from checking Irish to generating it. Checkers push writers toward the norm. Generators supply a model of the language whose quality depends on training text that practitioners describe as heavily polluted with machine translation.

Where the evidence stops

Five gaps that matter most

The repository lists eighteen. These are ranked by how much is missing against how feasible the work is.

GapWhy it is openWhat would close it
Model errors against learner errorsModels and fluent humans fail on different grammar contrasts. Nobody has compared the model pattern with the learner pattern.Line up benchmark categories with published learner error categories. No new human data needed.
Training-data provenanceMost of the Irish Wikipedia was reportedly written by two non-fluent editors, and it feeds Irish language models.Sample the sources and have fluent raters estimate the non-fluent and machine-translated share.
Speaker backgroundOnline corpora record text and nothing about the writer.Donated writing with a short background questionnaire.
Sound versus textThe best-documented new-speaker features are in pronunciation. All the computational evidence is about spelling and vocabulary.Acoustic measurement in learner recordings that speech projects already hold, with consent.
Generative AI in Irish-medium classroomsA government advisory paper warns of a divide for Gaelscoileanna. No study has observed what pupils and teachers do.Survey plus classroom observation, with ethics approval.

Limits

The data is old and from one platform

The tweets date from 2009 to 2019. Young people's Irish now lives on video and private messaging, where no corpus exists.

The counts are small

The mutation result rests on 25 unmarked cases in total. Intervals are wide.

The rules assume the written standard

Legitimate dialect forms can be flagged. The word lists are short and were built by a non-speaker.

There is no single "Gaelscoil Irish"

In the main study of pupil speech, error rates ranged from 20.5% to 41.6% across schools, and native-speaker Irish is itself changing.

No children's data, by design

Nothing here was collected from or about children, no platform was scraped, and only aggregate statistics are published. The feature extractor is not suitable for grading writing or profiling people.

Search coverage

The literature search ran in English-language indexes and will have missed work published in Irish. Sources read only in part are marked in the paper.

Open code

Reproduce it

Python 3.9 or later, standard library only. The treebanks download from their own repositories under their own licences.

git clone https://github.com/DenC-dev/irish-lang-research
cd irish-lang-research
./scripts/fetch_ud.sh
python3 -I scripts/compare_treebanks.py --idt external/UD_Irish-IDT \
    --twittirish external/UD_Irish-TwittIrish --out results
python3 -I scripts/compare_cohorts.py --twittirish external/UD_Irish-TwittIrish --out results
python3 -I scripts/clause_density.py --idt external/UD_Irish-IDT \
    --twittirish external/UD_Irish-TwittIrish --out results
